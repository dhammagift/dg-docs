# Principles of Translation

*See also the [glossary](/glossary), a searchable table of terms with the canon definition where one exists; [Rationale](/rationale), the detailed reasoning and examples behind these principles; and [Principles by the Suttas](/sutta), the sutta passages they rest on. This page is translated from the Russian original; some linguistic examples are best understood with reference to the source language (see the <SiteLink to="/ru/docs/principles">Russian version</SiteLink>).*

These principles and approaches apply wherever a translation or edit on Dhamma.gift is marked **"o"**: "translation o" (a text fully translated from Pāli) or "ed. o" (a text fragment amended against the Pāli original according to these Principles).

## In the Middle: Between Adaptation and Grammatical Analysis

Consistency, unified terminology and attention to the global context make it possible to see the Buddha's teaching with greater depth and subtlety. A literary adaptation built on narrowly contextual translation cannot do this.

A precise translation works as a necessary "bridge." It lets readers perceive the original structure of the text and the logic of the Dhamma directly, with little or no immersion in linguistics. It fills a missing link for those who want greater precision and independence in studying the primary sources but have not yet learned Pāli to the required level. This approach does **not** suggest that all translations should be done this way. Its aim is to fill the empty space between the many literary adaptations and dense grammatical analyses.

## Principles

Each principle is given in the same form: **Principle** (the formulation), **Grounding** (the sutta passage it rests on; where no direct sutta statement exists, the reason it is adopted is stated instead), and **Example**. The order reflects priority: definitions (3–4) come before systematic choice (5–6), and both come before tradition (7) and form (8–15).

### 1. Every translation is a work in progress

**Principle.** Any translation from Pāli, or translation of another author's translation, as of today (<Today />) is not considered final but is regarded as **"Work in Progress."** This holds whether it is a complete text, a fragment, a dictionary entry, a topical article or a single term, and whether it is digital, printed or oral. A final translation requires meanings and terms to be correctly established. If such a translation is possible at all, it can only be made by an Arahant with the four "analytical knowledges" (<SiteLink to="/an4.172:0.3?s=pa%E1%B9%ADisambhid&lang=en&hide=id">catasso paṭisambhidā</SiteLink>).

**Grounding.** AN 4.172 (the four *paṭisambhidā*); MN 95 on safeguarding the truth: "such is my faith," not "this alone is true" ([Principles by the Suttas](/sutta), principle 5).

**Example.** Renderings are revised when new contexts turn up (see *pariññāta* in [Rationale](/rationale#fully-understood-not-quite-full)).

### 2. Terminology is consistent

**Principle.** Terminology in the Buddha's Teaching must be **consistent**. Consistency is an integral part of any adequate set of instructions or documents, all the more so for the Supreme Teacher and the Supreme Teaching, which describes the most subtle, fundamental principles of the universe's workings and the methods of its realization. The Dhamma is **not** fiction. Meanings and definitions are uniform across all texts, especially those concerning the Four Noble Truths and the layers of text closest to them.

**Grounding.** The Dhamma is "well formulated" (*svākkhāto*, <SiteLink to="/an3.70:7.4?lang=en&hide=id">an3.70:7.4</SiteLink>). The Tathāgata's speech does not "go two ways" (*dvayagāminī*, <SiteLink to="/dn24:1.18.1?s=dvayag%C4%81min%C4%AB&lang=en&hide=id">dn24:1.18.1</SiteLink>).

**Example.** *Dukkha* is "pain" in the first truth, in the feeling triad and in the list of faculties alike ([Rationale: cross-verifiability](/rationale#cross-verifiability-of-consistent-terminology)).

### 3. A definition in the suttas is applied

**Principle.** If a concept has a definition in the suttas, that meaning is applied. Ignoring direct definitions of terms in the suttas is a sub-optimal approach: it renders the whole of the Buddha's Teaching not well formulated (not *svākkhāto*).

**Grounding.** AN 4.180: what is presented as the Teaching is checked against the suttas and the Vinaya (*sutte otāretabbāni, vinaye sandassetabbāni*, <SiteLink to="/an4.180:2.6?lang=en&hide=id">an4.180:2.6</SiteLink>). AN 2.24: presenting a discourse of explicit meaning (*nītattha*) as one needing interpretation (*neyyattha*) misrepresents the Tathāgata.

**Example.** *Dukkha* has a definition in the suttas: "And what is pain (*dukkha*)? Bodily pain, bodily discomfort…" (*Analysis of the Truths*, <SiteLink to="/mn141:16.1?s=dukkha&lang=en&hide=id">mn141:16.1–16.3</SiteLink>; see [Rationale](/rationale#dukkha)). In practice, when the suttas use *dukkha* (bodily pain, bodily discomfort), they point the reader to phenomena at the level of *rūpa* and *vedanā*. In translations that lack consistent terminology and render *dukkha* as "suffering," the reader's attention shifts to the level of *saññā* and *saṅkhārā* instead.

### 4. Failing the suttas, a definition in the Vinaya is applied

**Principle.** If a definition cannot be found in the suttas but can be found in the Vinaya, that meaning is applied. Here "Vinaya" means the Suttavibhaṅga with its word-by-word analysis (*padabhājanīya*).

**Grounding.** AN 4.180 names the Vinaya, alongside the suttas, as a criterion of verification.

**Example.** A term that the suttas use without defining is looked up in the word-analysis of the Suttavibhaṅga before any later source is consulted (cf. the "Vinaya" hits in the [glossary](/glossary)).

### 5. Without a definition, a systematic rendering is preferred

**Principle.** If a word's definition is found neither in the suttas nor in the Vinaya, a precise **systematic translation** is preferred over a narrowly contextual one.[^1] Ideally this is one word that covers as fully as possible all contexts of use in all or most occurrences in the suttas and the Vinaya.

**Grounding.** Methodological consequence of principle 2. Against a narrowly contextual practice, cf. AN 5.156: discourses learned "with words and phrases misplaced" lose their meaning.

**Example.** *Anta* is rendered "end" (edge, boundary) everywhere, in SN 56.11 as in AN 6.61, and not "extreme" in one sutta and "end" in another ([Rationale](/rationale#anta)).

### 6. Rare words: the global contextual or dictionary meaning

**Principle.** If a word occurs too rarely, or only in uniform contexts, and has no definition in the suttas or the Vinaya, so that a systematic rendering cannot be established, its global contextual or dictionary meaning is used.

**Grounding.** Methodological: the texts give too few uses to establish a systematic rendering.

**Example.** Names of plants, animals or utensils that occur once or twice are translated by their dictionary sense.

### 7. Tradition does not override the principles above

**Principle.** If a generally accepted translation or interpretation, including one from a commentary or sub-commentary, contradicts the points above, it is not applied.

**Grounding.** AN 3.65: do not go by oral tradition, lineage, scripture or the authority of a teacher (<SiteLink to="/an3.65:4.1?lang=en&hide=id">an3.65:4.1</SiteLink>). AN 2.23: do not ascribe to the Tathāgata what he did not say.

**Example.** "Suffering" for *dukkha* and "intention" for *cetanā* are established renderings. Being established does not by itself make them applicable ([Rationale](/rationale#dukkha)).

### 8. A broader Pāli concept: a word cluster

**Principle.** If a Pāli concept is broader than any single English concept, a word cluster (a hyphenated group of near-synonyms) or one word from the cluster is used. The cluster may appear at the first occurrence in a text; afterwards only the key word is used.

**Grounding.** MN 139: one should not override the normal designation (*samaññaṁ nātidhāveyya*, <SiteLink to="/mn139:3.9?lang=en&hide=id">mn139:3.9</SiteLink>). The English word keeps its ordinary meaning. Breadth is conveyed by adding words, not by stretching one word.

**Example.** *rāga*: fascination-passion. *anicca*: unreliability, instability, untrustworthiness. This is as if it had been written: fascination-passion (hereinafter *fascination*). Or *jhāyatha*: ignite (ardently strive, concentrate, "meditate," contemplate), hereinafter *ignite*.

### 9. A narrower Pāli concept: a clarifying addition

**Principle.** If a Pāli concept is more specific and the English concept broader, a clarifying addition is used.

**Grounding.** As for principle 8 (MN 139).

**Example.** *ceto*: mind-thinking; *citta*: mind-state; *mano*: mind-representation. This is as if it had been written: mind-state (hereinafter *mind*).

### 10. Pāli word order is preferably preserved

**Principle.** Preserving the word order of the Pāli sentence is preferred.

**Grounding.** AN 5.156 (parallel AN 4.160): misplaced words and phrases lead the meaning astray (*dunnikkhittehi padabyañjanehi*, <SiteLink to="/an5.156:1.3?lang=en&hide=id">an5.156:1.3</SiteLink>).

**Example.** *Ete kho, bhikkhave, ubho ante anupagamma majjhimā paṭipadā…* is rendered "Not approaching, monks, these two ends, the Middle Practice…" (sn56.11:2.4).

### 11. Pāli grammatical structure is preferably preserved

**Principle.** Preserving the grammatical structure of the Pāli is preferred: case, number and voice, and the distinction between noun and adjective.

**Grounding.** As for principle 10 (AN 5.156).

**Example.** *jātipi dukkhā* is rendered "birth is painful," with an adjective agreeing with *jāti*, and not "birth is suffering," with a noun ([Rationale](/rationale#dukkha)).

### 12. Prefixes and endings are translated consistently

**Principle.** Consistent, systematic translation of prefixes and endings is preferred where possible.

**Grounding.** Methodological consequence of principles 2 and 11.

**Example.** The prefix *saṁ-* in *sañcetanā* ("devising") is kept distinct from the simple *cetanā* ("cognitive processing"), so that the two terms do not merge ([Rationale](/rationale#kamma)). For the privative prefix see principle 13.

### 13. Negative prefixes are kept, not replaced by antonyms

**Principle.** Preserving the structure of Pāli words with the negative (privative) prefix *a-/an-* is preferred over replacing them with antonyms.

**Grounding.** The suttas themselves build such pairs, e.g. *abyāpādasaṅkappa* in the definition of right intention (<SiteLink to="/sn45.8:4.2?lang=en&hide=id">sn45.8:4.2</SiteLink>).

**Example.** *byāpāda* and *abyāpāda* are rendered "hostility" and "non-hostility," not "hostility" and "friendliness."

### 14. The structure of compounds is preserved

**Principle.** Preserving the structure of compounds (*samāsa*) as in Pāli is preferred. The members are joined by hyphens. Linking words ("of," "for," "and") are added only where the form without them would sound completely unnatural in English. Leaving the relation between the members unspecified keeps the compound open and polysemous.[^2]

**Grounding.** Methodological. Pāli itself distinguishes a compound from the corresponding phrase, as in *Āhārasamudayā kāyassa samudayo* (<SiteLink to="/sn47.42:1.4?lang=en&hide=id">sn47.42:1.4</SiteLink>; see note 2).

**Example.** *ariyasacca*, "Noble-Truth," leaves the case relation open: it can be read as a genitive, dative or ablative *tatpuruṣa*, or as a *karmadhāraya*. It is at once the truth of the Noble, a truth leading to nobility, a truth from the nobles, a truth for becoming noble and a truth for those already noble.

### 15. Under semantic divergence, the Pāli meaning decides

**Principle.** Where meanings diverge, the translation is based on the meaning of the Pāli term, not on its outward similarity to an English equivalent. The same rule applies to "false friends of the translator."[^3]

**Grounding.** MN 103: where the meaning (*attha*) does not agree, it is worth sorting out (<SiteLink to="/mn103:11.2?lang=en&hide=id">mn103:11.2</SiteLink>).

**Example.** *Taṇhā* and "thirst" have similar denotations but different connotations. "Thirst" is loud and result-focused, whereas *taṇhā* is "accompanied by joy-passion, delighting here and there" ([Rationale](/rationale#semantic-divergence)).

[^1]: **Systematic translation (consistent, concordant terminology)** is strictly grounded in the direct definitions of terms found in the primary sources (the Suttas and the Vinaya). The verified meaning is applied consistently and universally across the entire corpus. **Narrowly contextual (situational) translation** is the opposite approach. The strict definitions from the Suttas are ignored, and the meaning of a single word changes, sometimes drastically, from text to text, driven by tradition, the translator's taste or audience expectations. See [Rationale](/rationale#issues-with-narrowly-contextual-translation) for a detailed breakdown.
[^2]: In addition to the standard understanding of Pāli compounds (see e.g. [Pali Compounds](https://www.digitalpalireader.online/docs/pali/compound.htm)), the project proposes a broader view. A dash in a date range ("1–10") does not mean only "1 and 10." In the same way, a Pāli copulative compound (*dvandva*) such as *samaṇabrāhmaṇā*, "ascetics-brahmins," need not mean only "ascetics and brahmins," although both forms occur in the texts. It can mean the whole spectrum of the spiritual sphere from ascetics to brahmins, just as *nandīrāga* covers the whole spectrum from joy to passion, not only "joy and passion." On this view, splitting a Pāli compound for ease of translation can in some cases introduce distortions. Compare "water runs through a water-pipe," which loses something when forced apart into "water" and "pipe" taken separately. A case in point from Pāli: *Āhārasamudayā kāyassa samudayo*, "from nourishment-arising, the arising of the body" (<SiteLink to="/sn47.42:1.4?lang=en&hide=id">sn47.42:1.4</SiteLink>). The example is subtler than the water-pipe one, but it shows that the Pāli texts distinguish compounds from their "split" counterparts, although it is commonly assumed that there is no such difference. English has no case endings to drop inside a compound. Its equivalent device is to join the members with hyphens and leave out "of" and "and" wherever the result stays readable, e.g. "suffering-lamentation-pain-discontent-despair."
[^3]: False friends of the translator are words of two languages that are similar in form but differ in meaning, fully or partly. Examples: English *aggressive* ("hostile," but also "energetic, assertive") vs. Russian *агрессивный* (only "hostile"); English *sympathy* ("compassion") vs. Russian *симпатия* ("liking, attraction"). See [Rationale](/rationale#semantic-divergence).
