# Dhamma Principles

<img src={VijjaHatthipada} alt="Dhamma Principles" className="dg-hero-image" />

<PageTools />

The "o" translations, and the approach Dhamma.gift offers as a whole, rest on
the points of the Buddha's Teaching from the Suttas below. These are the
criteria the Buddha recommended for verifying teachings, and at the same time
a practical checklist for recognizing the errors and distortions found in the
vast majority of existing traditions and translations.

## Textual authenticity — the Four Great References

**AN 4.180 (Mahāpadesa Sutta)** — how to check a claim of "this is the
Dhamma, this is the Vinaya, this is the Teacher's word": check it against
the suttas and the Vinaya, rather than accepting or rejecting it on the
authority of whoever reported it (the Sutta makes clear that individual
monks and whole communities can pass on what is not the Teacher's Teaching,
and verifying it takes a check against the Suttas and the Vinaya). <SuttaLink index={0}>Read →</SuttaLink>

**AN 1.132–139**: monks who present what the Realized One did not say as said by him, and what he said as not said, act for the harm of many and make the true Dhamma disappear. So before saying "the Buddha taught this," find where it is said. If a claim is found only in the commentaries, the Abhidhamma or later texts, it cannot be passed off as the Teacher's word. <SuttaLink index={16}>Read →</SuttaLink>

## Buddha, Dhamma, Sangha: keys to reading

The three recollections are not only a religious form. The qualities with which the suttas describe the Buddha, the Dhamma and the Sangha work as criteria in their own right: a reading that contradicts them is in doubt.

**So and not otherwise.** Everything the Realized One says between the night of awakening and the night of final nibbāna "is just so and not otherwise" (<SiteLink to="/an4.23:3.1?lang=en&hide=id">AN 4.23</SiteLink>; <SiteLink to="/dn29:29.3?lang=en&hide=id">DN 29</SiteLink>). What is said in one sutta does not diverge from what is said in another, so the suttas can be checked against each other. For a term this means: its meaning does not change from sutta to sutta silently. Where a sense is figurative or special, the suttas usually say so openly (see «A term» below). <SuttaLink index={33}>Read →</SuttaLink>

**Buddha.** The recollection of the Buddha calls him rightly awakened, knower of the world, teacher of gods and humans (<SiteLink to="/an6.10:2.3?lang=en&hide=id">AN 6.10</SiteLink>). As the teacher after him he left the Dhamma and Vinaya (<SiteLink to="/dn16:6.1.5?lang=en&hide=id">DN 16</SiteLink>), so a definition he gave is the measure for a later interpretation, not the other way round.

**Dhamma.** The recollection of the Dhamma: "the Dhamma is well formulated by the Blessed One, visible here and now, timeless, inviting to come and see, leading onward, to be known personally by the wise" (<SiteLink to="/an6.10:3.2?lang=en&hide=id">AN 6.10</SiteLink>). Analysis and explanation are still needed, and the Buddha himself approves them (see «A disciple's analysis» below). But analysis unfolds what is formulated, it does not correct it. Visible here and now, known personally by the wise: a right reading can be checked by experience, it does not rest on a commentary alone. <SuttaLink index={31}>Read →</SuttaLink>

**No patchwork.** "So well formulated by me is the Dhamma, opened up, made clear, with the patchwork cut away" (*chinnapilotiko*, <SiteLink to="/mn22:42.1?lang=en&hide=id">MN 22</SiteLink>; <SiteLink to="/sn12.22:2.1?lang=en&hide=id">SN 12.22</SiteLink>). The sutta says this of the Dhamma as a whole: it is open and not stitched from pieces. Our conclusion for translation: if a word has to be given different meanings the suttas themselves do not speak of, stitched together by interpretations, the translation brings into the Dhamma a patchwork that is not there. DN 29 calls the teaching complete, with nothing lacking and nothing in excess (*anūnamanadhikaṁ*, <SiteLink to="/dn29:16.5?lang=en&hide=id">DN 29</SiteLink>). <SuttaLink index={32}>Read →</SuttaLink>

**The Dhamma as the teacher.** "Some of you might think: 'The Teacher's word is past, we have no Teacher.' But you should not see it like this. The Dhamma and Vinaya I have taught and laid down for you will be your Teacher after my passing" (<SiteLink to="/dn16:6.1.2?lang=en&hide=id">DN 16</SiteLink>). When Ānanda is asked who their refuge is, since the Buddha appointed no monk: "we have a refuge: the Dhamma is our refuge" (<SiteLink to="/mn108:9.3?lang=en&hide=id">MN 108</SiteLink>). Our conclusion: a Dhamma that cannot be understood without an interpreter would not be a teacher; the interpreter would become the teacher. And the suttas do say themselves which word is meant where: *dukkhindriya* is bodily pain (<SiteLink to="/sn48.36:3.2?lang=en&hide=id">SN 48.36</SiteLink>); *dukkhā vedanā* is a painful feeling, bodily or mental (<SiteLink to="/mn44:23.4?lang=en&hide=id">MN 44</SiteLink>); the first noble truth says of birth, ageing, illness, death and, in short, the five aggregates of sustaining that they are *dukkha*, painful (<SiteLink to="/sn56.11:4.2?lang=en&hide=id">SN 56.11</SiteLink>).

**Sangha.** The Sangha of disciples practises the straight and true way (*ujuppaṭipanno, ñāyappaṭipanno*): the four pairs, the eight kinds of persons (<SiteLink to="/an6.10:4.2?lang=en&hide=id">AN 6.10</SiteLink>), that is, the noble disciples. The way is called straight in the suttas themselves: "that path is called the straight one" (*ujuko nāma so maggo*, <SiteLink to="/sn1.46:2.1?lang=en&hide=id">SN 1.46</SiteLink>). Our conclusion: a straight way is hard to keep by a reading in which a key word changes its sense where the suttas themselves say nothing of it. How a disciple's word relates to the Teacher's is the subject of the next section.

## The island: the Dhamma and one's own observation

"Live as an island to yourselves, a refuge to yourselves, with no other refuge; with the Dhamma as island, the Dhamma as refuge, with no other refuge" (<SiteLink to="/dn16:2.26.1?lang=en&hide=id">DN 16</SiteLink>; <SiteLink to="/sn47.13:6.9?lang=en&hide=id">SN 47.13</SiteLink>). The sutta says at once how this is done: observing the body in the body, feelings in feelings, the mind in the mind, phenomena in phenomena (<SiteLink to="/sn47.13:7.1?lang=en&hide=id">SN 47.13</SiteLink>). The island here is not an opinion or a tradition, but the Dhamma and one's own observation.

In SN 22.43 one who lives as an island examines the root itself: where sorrow, lamentation, pain, displeasure and distress are born from (<SiteLink to="/sn22.43:1.3?lang=en&hide=id">SN 22.43</SiteLink>). After the Buddha's passing, the Dhamma and Vinaya he taught remain as the teacher (<SiteLink to="/dn16:6.1.5?lang=en&hide=id">DN 16</SiteLink>). Our conclusion: a reading that rests only on an interpreter's authority, not on the Dhamma and observation, in effect makes the interpreter another refuge. <SuttaLink index={34}>Read →</SuttaLink>

Friends are not superfluous here. Ānanda calls good friendship half of the holy life, and the Buddha corrects him: not half, the whole holy life (<SiteLink to="/sn45.2:2.2?lang=en&hide=id">SN 45.2</SiteLink>). But he names the chief good friend himself: "relying on me as a good friend", beings are freed from birth, ageing, death, from sorrow, lamentation, pain, displeasure and distress (<SiteLink to="/sn45.2:4.2?lang=en&hide=id">SN 45.2</SiteLink>). Friends help, and the measure of their words is the same: the Dhamma. <SuttaLink index={35}>Read →</SuttaLink>

## A disciple's analysis confirmed by the Buddha

A disciple's words carry the weight of the Teacher's when the Buddha himself confirmed them. Of Mahākaccāna's analysis he says: if you asked me, I would explain it in just the same way (<SiteLink to="/mn18:21.2?lang=en&hide=id">MN 18</SiteLink>). The same of the nun Dhammadinnā's analysis (<SiteLink to="/mn44:30.5?lang=en&hide=id">MN 44</SiteLink>). In MN 141 the Buddha says beforehand that Sāriputta is able to set out and analyse the four noble truths in detail (<SiteLink to="/mn141:5.7?lang=en&hide=id">MN 141</SiteLink>), and the rest of the sutta is that analysis.

Where there is no such confirmation, a disciple's words are a second-rank argument: they are checked against the suttas in the same way, by AN 4.180.

## The national language doesn't need to change

**MN 139 (Araṇavibhaṅga Sutta)** — a great many points in translations of
the Dhamma violate this very principle. English words such as *suffering*,
*craving*, *arising*, *cessation*, *form*, and *intention* have been given
modified — either broadened or narrowed — meanings within "translated
Buddhism," which breaks the principle stated by the Buddha himself: the
receiving language doesn't need to be reshaped, and phenomena should be
called what they would ordinarily be called in that language — in our
case, English. More on this in the [Rationale](/rationale). <SuttaLink index={3}>Read →</SuttaLink>

**MN 103 (Kinti Sutta)** — when the meaning (*attha*) agrees but the
wording (*byañjana*) differs, that is not something to quarrel over;
disputes over minor phrasing devalue an otherwise correct teaching. But a
mismatch in meaning does matter — even at the cost of some friction between
both sides, it is worth clarifying what was poorly grasped as poorly
grasped, and what was well grasped as well grasped. <SuttaLink index={4}>Read →</SuttaLink>

**DN 29 (Pāsādika Sutta)**: if a fellow practitioner passes on the Dhamma with the meaning, the wording or both wrong, he is neither approved nor rejected but asked: "for this meaning, which wording fits better, this or that? for this wording, which meaning fits better?" (*opāyikatara*, <SiteLink to="/dn29:18.5?lang=en&hide=id">DN 29</SiteLink>). If both meaning and wording are right, what he said is approved (<SiteLink to="/dn29:21.4?lang=en&hide=id">DN 29</SiteLink>). This gives four cases for any translation: meaning and wording right; meaning right, wording wrong; wording right, meaning wrong; both wrong. <SuttaLink index={28}>Read →</SuttaLink>

**DN 9**: the names, expressions, designations and descriptions of the world are what the Realized One uses, without holding on to them (*lokasamaññā lokaniruttiyo lokavohārā lokapaññattiyo, yāhi tathāgato voharati aparāmasaṁ*). He speaks in ordinary words in their ordinary sense. <SuttaLink index={24}>Read →</SuttaLink>

**SN 22.62 (Niruttipatha Sutta)**: the three pathways of naming are unmixed and will not be mixed: of the past one says "was", of the future "will be", of the present "is" (<SiteLink to="/sn22.62:1.4?lang=en&hide=id">SN 22.62</SiteLink>, <SiteLink to="/sn22.62:5.1?lang=en&hide=id">5.1</SiteLink>, <SiteLink to="/sn22.62:9.1?lang=en&hide=id">9.1</SiteLink>). Even those who deny causes and the nihilists did not reject these designations (<SiteLink to="/sn22.62:13.2?lang=en&hide=id">SN 22.62</SiteLink>). The Buddha uses them in their ordinary sense. <SuttaLink index={21}>Read →</SuttaLink>

**SN 22.94**: "I do not dispute with the world, monks; it is the world that disputes with me." What the wise in the world hold not to exist, the Buddha too says does not exist; what they hold to exist, he too says exists (<SiteLink to="/sn22.94:1.4?lang=en&hide=id">SN 22.94</SiteLink>). <SuttaLink index={25}>Read →</SuttaLink>

**Cullavagga (Kd 15)**: two monks proposed to put the Buddha's word into verse metre (*chandaso*), because monks of various clans "spoil it in their own dialect." The Buddha did not allow it, and allowed the Buddha's word to be learned *sakāya niruttiyā*, "in one's own dialect" (<SiteLink to="/pli-tv-kd15:33.1.15?lang=en&hide=id">Kd 15</SiteLink>). Whose dialect is meant is read in different ways. But recasting the teaching in an elevated form for the sake of beauty the Buddha did not allow. <SuttaLink index={26}>Read →</SuttaLink>

## A term: direct, metaphorical, figurative and special sense in the noble one's discipline

The suttas use words in different ways and usually say themselves which.

- **Direct definition.** It has several formulas, and all of them are direct:
  - "what is X? … this is called X" (*Katamañca … ? … idaṁ vuccati …*): so the analysis of the first truth defines birth, ageing, pain, displeasure and the rest (<SiteLink to="/dn22:18.18?lang=en&hide=id">DN 22</SiteLink>; the whole row <SiteLink to="/dn22:18.3?lang=en&hide=id">DN 22</SiteLink>);
  - the word said twice, "'X, X', so it is said. What then is X?" (*‘Nibbānaṁ, nibbānan’ti, āvuso sāriputta, vuccati. Katamaṁ nu kho, āvuso, nibbānan’ti?*), with the answer "this is called nibbāna" (<SiteLink to="/sn38.1:2.1?lang=en&hide=id">SN 38.1</SiteLink>);
  - "'X, X', so it is said. To what extent is it called X?" (*‘Saññā saññā’ti, āvuso, vuccati. Kittāvatā nu kho, āvuso, saññāti vuccati?*, <SiteLink to="/mn43:8.1?lang=en&hide=id">MN 43</SiteLink>);
  - "how is X? … so is X" (*Kathañca … ? … Evaṁ kho …*, <SiteLink to="/sn47.13:7.1?lang=en&hide=id">SN 47.13</SiteLink>, <SiteLink to="/sn47.13:7.6?lang=en&hide=id">7.6</SiteLink>).
- **Simile** (*seyyathāpi … evameva kho*, "just as … so too"). The Buddha says himself that it is a simile: "I have made this simile to convey the meaning" (*upamā kho myāyaṁ, bhikkhave, katā atthassa viññāpanāya*, <SiteLink to="/sn35.238:7.1?lang=en&hide=id">SN 35.238</SiteLink>), and explains each part of it.
- **Designation** (*adhivacana*). A figurative name is called a designation: "'danger', monks, is a designation for desires" (*‘Bhayan’ti, bhikkhave, kāmānametaṁ adhivacanaṁ*), and then it says why (<SiteLink to="/an6.23:1.1?lang=en&hide=id">AN 6.23</SiteLink>, <SiteLink to="/an6.23:2.2?lang=en&hide=id">2.2</SiteLink>).
- **Conditional and direct sense** (*pariyāyena / nippariyāyena*). Ānanda examines one expression: "to this extent the Blessed One spoke of it in a conditional sense" (*vutto bhagavatā pariyāyena*), and at the end: "in a direct sense" (*nippariyāyena*, <SiteLink to="/an9.42:4.2?lang=en&hide=id">AN 9.42</SiteLink>, <SiteLink to="/an9.42:12.2?lang=en&hide=id">12.2</SiteLink>).
- **In the noble one's discipline** (*ariyassa vinaye*). A special sense is named openly, often by contrast: "the brahmins have it one way, the noble one's discipline another" (*aññathā kho, brāhmaṇa, brāhmaṇā … paññāpenti, aññathā ca pana ariyassa vinaye …*, <SiteLink to="/an3.58:3.1?lang=en&hide=id">AN 3.58</SiteLink>); "what disintegrates is called the world in the noble one's discipline" (*yaṁ kho, ānanda, palokadhammaṁ, ayaṁ vuccati ariyassa vinaye loko*, <SiteLink to="/sn35.84:2.3?lang=en&hide=id">SN 35.84</SiteLink>).

So a special sense of a term is looked for where the suttas speak of it, not introduced on one's own. <SuttaLink index={36}>Read →</SuttaLink>

## A term's scope and checking its meaning

**SN 35.23 (Sabba Sutta)**: the Buddha defines "the all": the eye and sights, the ear and sounds, the nose and smells, the tongue and tastes, the body and touches, the mind and phenomena. Whoever says "rejecting this all, I will describe another all" would have mere words (*vācāvatthukamevassa*): asked, he could not explain it and would get into trouble, because it is outside his range (<SiteLink to="/sn35.23:1.8?lang=en&hide=id">SN 35.23</SiteLink>). The sutta says this of "the all". We draw a parallel: a term the suttas define themselves is in the same position. Whoever rejects the given scope and describes "another" is no longer speaking of what the sutta speaks of. <SuttaLink index={20}>Read →</SuttaLink>

**AN 7.68 (Dhammaññū Sutta)**: "one who knows the meaning" knows the meaning of each statement: "this is the meaning of this statement, this is the meaning of that" (<SiteLink to="/an7.68:3.7?lang=en&hide=id">AN 7.68</SiteLink>). For translation this means: a term is tested not in one convenient place but in every place it stands. If a rendering yields nonsense somewhere, it needs revising. <SuttaLink index={22}>Read →</SuttaLink>

**AN 2.47**: in a worthy assembly, having learned what the Realized One said, they ask each other: "how is this? what is the meaning of this?" (*imassa ko attho*, <SiteLink to="/an2.47:2.5?lang=en&hide=id">AN 2.47</SiteLink>). In an unworthy one they learn the polished compositions of poets and do not question each other (<SiteLink to="/an2.47:1.6?lang=en&hide=id">AN 2.47</SiteLink>). The meaning is sought by questioning what the Realized One himself said. <SuttaLink index={23}>Read →</SuttaLink>

## Criteria of "effectiveness" — not authority, not hearsay

**AN 3.65 (Kesamutti / Kālāma Sutta)** — the companion piece to Mahāpadesa:
don't accept a teaching merely because of tradition, report, scripture,
logic, inference, superficial plausibility, agreement with a view you
already hold, the speaker's apparent competence, or "this ascetic is our
teacher." Test it by whether it leads to the skillful or the unskillful, to
harm or to benefit. <SuttaLink index={1}>Read →</SuttaLink>

**MN 95 (Caṅkī Sutta)** — the companion to Kālāma: where Kālāma says what
*not* to infer a teaching's truth from, MN 95 says what one may honestly say
instead. Safeguarding the truth (*saccānurakkhaṇa*) is to say "such is my
faith," not "this alone is true, anything else is wrong." <SuttaLink index={2}>Read →</SuttaLink>

## Scrutinize before you praise or blame

**AN 4.3** — praising or condemning something without first examining and
investigating it (*ananuvicca apariyogāhetvā*) is listed as a failing;
examining first is the mark of the wise. <SuttaLink index={5}>Read →</SuttaLink>

## Definitive vs. interpretable meaning

**AN 2.24 / AN 2.25** — misrepresenting a discourse whose meaning is already
explicit (*nītattha*) as one that still needs interpreting (*neyyattha*),
or vice versa, is exactly how mistranslation and misreading happen in
practice.

## How a question should be answered

**AN 4.42** — four ways of answering a question (*vibhajjabyākaraṇa* among
them): some questions should be answered directly, some need to be
analyzed/qualified, some should be turned back with a counter-question, and
some should be set aside. This is an important aspect of the
Dhamma, and one of the criteria that, for example, helps establish that
*anicca* should not be translated as "impermanence."[^1] <SuttaLink index={9}>Read →</SuttaLink>

**AN 3.67 (Kathāvatthu Sutta)**: how a person takes part in a discussion shows whether he is fit to discuss with. He is unfit if he does not answer categorically a question to be answered categorically, or with analysis one to be answered with analysis (<SiteLink to="/an3.67:2.2?lang=en&hide=id">AN 3.67</SiteLink>). He is unfit too if he does not stand by what is possible and impossible, by agreed assumptions, by known teachings and by the procedure of the discussion (<SiteLink to="/an3.67:3.2?lang=en&hide=id">3.2</SiteLink>). For a translation this is a test: does the sutta, so rendered, assert what other suttas call impossible, and does one meaning of the term hold from the start of the argument to its end?

The same sutta names marks of conduct: jumping from one thing to another, leading the talk astray, anger (<SiteLink to="/an3.67:4.2?lang=en&hide=id">4.2</SiteLink>), browbeating, ridicule, seizing on slips (<SiteLink to="/an3.67:5.2?lang=en&hide=id">5.2</SiteLink>). This is conduct, not refutation. Whoever lends an ear draws near the purpose of discussion: the liberation of mind through not sustaining (<SiteLink to="/an3.67:6.2?lang=en&hide=id">6.2–6.8</SiteLink>). <SuttaLink index={30}>Read →</SuttaLink>

## How the Dhamma disappears over time

**SN 20.7 (Āṇi Sutta)** — the simile of the drum repaired peg by peg until
none of the original wood remains: discourses "spoken by the Realized One,
deep, profound in meaning, transcendent" will stop being learned, while
literary compositions — "composed by poets, disciple-followers" — even
today often carry more authority than the suttas themselves. <SuttaLink index={10}>Read →</SuttaLink>

**SN 16.13 (Saddhammappatirūpaka Sutta)** — where SN 20.7 shows *how* the
true teaching fades, this shows *why*: it does not disappear while no
counterfeit of it (*saddhammappatirūpaka*) has arisen in the world — as
counterfeit gold appears, true gold vanishes. <SuttaLink index={11}>Read →</SuttaLink>

**AN 5.156** (parallel: AN 4.160) — discourses learned with words and
phrases misplaced, where the meaning goes astray. <SuttaLink index={12}>Read →</SuttaLink>

**AN 2.20**: two things lead to the decline and disappearance of the true Dhamma: badly placed words and phrases, and badly drawn meaning. "When words and phrases are badly placed, the meaning too is badly drawn" (<SiteLink to="/an2.20:1.4?lang=en&hide=id">AN 2.20</SiteLink>). Wording is a trifle as long as the meaning is the same (MN 103 above). When rearranged words, a lost particle or lost agreement lead the meaning astray, it is no longer a trifle. <SuttaLink index={27}>Read →</SuttaLink>

**AN 5.79**: a future danger: monks will not want to hear the suttas spoken by the Realized One, deep, deep in meaning, connected with emptiness, but will listen to polished compositions of poets, words of followers, and think them worth learning (<SiteLink to="/an5.79:5.2?lang=en&hide=id">AN 5.79</SiteLink>). If the only argument for a rendering is "that is customary," "that sounds better" or "that is how a well-known translator has it," it is not an argument. <SuttaLink index={29}>Read →</SuttaLink>

## What counts as "well spoken"

**AN 8.8** — *yaṁ kiñci subhāsitaṁ sabbaṁ taṁ tassa bhagavato vacanaṁ* —
"whatever is well said is all the word of the Blessed One." A useful check
against a narrower, more territorial reading of what counts as legitimate
Dhamma — while still being read together with Mahāpadesa and Kālāma above,
not as license for anything to be called Buddha-word. <SuttaLink index={13}>Read →</SuttaLink>

## Grasping the teaching correctly

**MN 22 (Alagaddūpama Sutta)** — the water-snake simile: learning the
Dhamma for the sake of debate and finding fault with others, rather than
for understanding, is learning it wrongly, and it works against the
learner rather than for them. <SuttaLink index={14}>Read →</SuttaLink>

## The Dhamma is not sung

**AN 5.209 (Gītassara Sutta)** — five dangers for one who recites the
Dhamma in a drawn-out, singing voice (*āyatakena gītassarena*): he is
charmed by his own voice, others are charmed by it, householders take
offence — "the ascetics sing just as we do", one who aims at a beautiful
voice breaks his samādhi, and the next generation follows the example.
<SuttaLink index={15}>Read →</SuttaLink>

## The four truths in depth — Hatthipada

As in <SiteLink to="/mn28:2.1?lang=en&hide=id">MN 28</SiteLink>: the footprints of all animals fit into the elephant's, and everything skilful fits into the four noble truths. <SiteLink to="/hatthipada/?lang=en">Hatthipada</SiteLink> shows how the suttas themselves define every word of the truths: click a word and its definitions open ("Katamā ca, bhikkhave, X?" … "idaṁ vuccati X"), their words click again, as deep as you go. Only the Pāḷi of the four nikāyas and the suttas' own formulas; the translation is a reference.

[^1]: An example: in <SiteLink to="/sn22.59:6.4?lang=en&hide=id">sn22.59</SiteLink> the Buddha asks: *Yaṁ panāniccaṁ dukkhaṁ vā taṁ sukhaṁ vā?* — "And what is anicca, is it painful or pleasant?" The monks answer categorically: *Dukkhaṁ, bhante* — "Painful, venerable sir," and the Buddha accepts that answer. So this is a question to be answered categorically (*ekaṁsabyākaraṇīya*). If anicca meant "impermanent," no categorical answer would be possible: what is impermanent is not always painful. An impermanent pain is unpleasant while it lasts and pleasant when it goes; something pleasant, on the contrary, is painful when it goes. Such a question would have to be answered with analysis (*vibhajjabyākaraṇa*): "it depends on what exactly is impermanent." The categorical answer the Buddha accepts shows that anicca is what is always painful: unreliable, unstable, what cannot be relied upon. This is a serious argument: in <SiteLink to="/an3.67:2.2?lang=en&hide=id">an3.67</SiteLink> the Buddha states plainly that one who answers categorically a question that calls for analysis (or the reverse) is not fit to talk with. By accepting a categorical answer to a question that calls for analysis, the Buddha would contradict his own words.
